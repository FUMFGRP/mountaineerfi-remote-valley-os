In Jumla, clean water is not guaranteed. Glacial melt carries sediment, surface water is contaminated by animal waste and human activity, and boiling every liter of drinking water is energy-intensive and unsustainable.

We spent three weeks testing four low-cost filtration designs in households across the district. This is what we learned.

## The Challenge

Jumla's water sources vary dramatically by season. During the monsoon, surface water is abundant but heavily contaminated. In winter, springs slow to a trickle and freeze overnight. Most households boil water for 20-30 minutes per batch — consuming precious firewood or kerosene.

## What We Tested

We brought four filter prototypes designed for local manufacture:

1. **Gravity-fed ceramic candle filter** — The simplest design. Two stacked buckets with a ceramic element. No moving parts.
2. **UV purification unit** — Solar-powered UV-C lamp for community centers. Higher throughput but more complex.
3. **Bio-sand filter** — Layers of sand and gravel with a biological layer. Low maintenance but requires space.
4. **Ceramic pot filter with silver coating** — Locally fired clay pots treated with colloidal silver. Promising but fragile.

## Results

The ceramic candle filter was the clear winner. After two weeks of daily use by a family of five, it delivered 10 liters of clean water per day at near-zero maintenance. The ceramic element cost less than $3 to produce locally.

The UV unit worked well but required reliable solar charging — a challenge during Jumla's monsoon season. The bio-sand filter was too bulky for household use but shows promise for schools and health posts.
